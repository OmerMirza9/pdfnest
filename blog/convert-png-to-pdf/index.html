2. Transparency: keep it or flatten it?

Transparency is the whole reason PNG exists — and the thing most converters get wrong. PDF actually supports transparent images, so there are three sensible choices:

  • Keep transparency — the image stays see-through in the PDF. Best for logos, stamps and signatures you'll place over other content.
  • Flatten onto white — the safe default for documents, screenshots and anything headed for print. What you see is exactly what everyone gets.
  • Flatten onto black — for light-on-transparent designs (a white logo, for instance) that would vanish on a white page.

The trap: many free converters flatten silently onto white — and a few onto black — without asking. If your design came out invisible or on the wrong background, that's why. Pick a tool that lets you decide.

4. Keeping screenshots pixel-sharp

Screenshots are mostly text — and text is exactly what JPG compression destroys. A converter that internally re-encodes your PNG as JPG will give you slightly smudged, ringed lettering in the PDF. A proper converter embeds the PNG's pixels losslessly, so the PDF looks identical to the original, zoom and all. If the resulting file is bigger than you'd like, compress it afterwards with the Compress PDF tool — that way you control the quality trade-off, not the converter.

5. PNG vs JPG for PDFs — which should you start from?

If you have both versions of an image, start from the right one: PNG for screenshots, scans of text, diagrams and anything with sharp edges or transparency; JPG for photographs, where its compression is invisible and the smaller size helps.
