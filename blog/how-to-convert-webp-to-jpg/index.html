How to Convert WebP to JPG

PBy the PDFNest Team·October 3, 2026·5 min read

You usually meet WebP by accident: you save an image from a website and end up with a file your photo app, upload form or printer will not take. The format itself is good — that is why sites use it — but support outside the browser is patchy. Converting takes seconds, and the only decision worth thinking about is what happens to transparency.

Why you ended up with a WebP file

WebP is an image format developed by Google to make web images smaller at comparable quality. It generally succeeds, which is why so many sites now serve it — and why saving an image from a page hands you a .webp rather than the .jpg you expected.

Browsers handle it universally, so the file looks fine when you open it in a tab. The friction appears everywhere else: an older photo editor, a document template, a print service, a council upload form or a piece of business software may simply refuse it. Those tools were written against JPG and PNG and never updated.

So the usual reason to convert is compatibility, not quality. You are not fixing a defective file; you are translating a good file into the format the thing you need to use actually accepts. That framing matters, because it means converting once, for that purpose, rather than converting your whole library on principle.

Convert WebP to JPG in your browser

Open Convert Image, drop the .webp file in, choose JPG as the output and download the result. The conversion runs in your browser — the image is never uploaded — which is worth knowing when the picture is a document scan, a screenshot of something private or a photo of a person.

Check the output before you rely on it. Open the JPG and compare it with the original at the size you will actually use: a web banner viewed at full screen, or a photo you will print. Conversion problems are almost always visible immediately, and almost always to do with transparency rather than detail.

Watch the filename too. Some sites hand out files named image.jpg that are really WebP inside, which is why an upload form rejects something that looks correctly named. If a file fails a check that its extension says it should pass, convert it properly rather than renaming it — renaming changes the label, never the contents.

Convert a whole batch at once

If you saved a dozen images from a site, convert them in one go rather than individually. Drop the whole set into Convert Image, pick JPG once, and each file is converted and downloaded with the same settings. The consistency matters as much as the time saved — mixed settings across a set of images show up later as images that do not quite match.

Convert from the originals every time. Converting file one to JPG, then using that JPG as the source for another conversion, stacks one lossy encode on another. Keep the .webp set until the JPGs are confirmed good, then delete the ones you do not need.

For a large batch, the browser is doing real work locally, so expect it to take a moment and keep the tab in the foreground. If the set is enormous, convert it in two or three groups rather than one — the result is identical and you avoid a stalled tab partway through. The files then bundle neatly with Create ZIP if you are sending them on.

What happens to transparency

This is the one real decision. WebP can store transparency; JPG cannot, at all. When a transparent image becomes a JPG, the see-through areas have to be filled with something solid, and the usual result is a white or black rectangle where you expected nothing.

It matters for logos, icons, badges, cut-out product shots and anything designed to sit on a coloured background. A logo converted to JPG arrives with a visible box around it, which is obvious the moment you place it on a non-white page — and not obvious at all while you are looking at it on a white one.

Quality and file size

Both formats are lossy, so re-encoding adds a small amount of loss. In a photograph it is very hard to see. In an image with flat colour, sharp edges or text — a screenshot, a diagram, a logo — JPG’s compression shows as faint haloes around the edges, which is a reason to choose PNG for that kind of content rather than a reason to avoid converting.

Expect the JPG to be somewhat larger than the WebP at similar quality. That is the whole point of WebP, and it is a fair price for compatibility, but it is worth knowing if the file is going somewhere with a size limit. A JPG that overshoots an upload cap can be reduced with Compress Image.

Resist repeated round trips. Each conversion between lossy formats degrades the image a little, and the losses accumulate in a way that is invisible step by step and obvious after five steps. Keep one good original, convert from it when you need a different format, and treat the converted copies as disposable.

When to keep the WebP

If the image is destined for a website you control, keep the WebP. It is smaller than the equivalent JPG at the same quality, every current browser displays it, and converting to JPG makes your pages heavier for no benefit. Converting for the web is a step backwards.

Keep it too when the original is the only copy you have. A WebP you convert and then delete cannot be recovered to its original quality, so retain the source until you are certain the JPG works where you need it. Storage is cheap; a re-download from a site that has since changed is not always possible.

Convert when something concrete requires it: an application that will not open the file, a form that rejects the format, a print service with a fixed list, or a colleague whose software is older than yours. That is a clear reason, and it tells you exactly which format to pick and how many files to bother with.

Frequently asked questions

Why will my WebP file not open in some apps?

WebP is well supported by browsers but not by every older application, printer driver or upload form. Those tools expect JPG or PNG, so converting is the practical fix.

Does converting WebP to JPG lose quality?

Slightly. Both formats are lossy, so re-encoding adds a small amount of loss. It is rarely visible in a photograph, but convert from the original rather than repeatedly.

What happens to a transparent background?

JPG cannot store transparency, so transparent areas are filled with a solid colour. If you need the transparency, convert to PNG instead.
